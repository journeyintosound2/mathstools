# Emerald Jungle — ten proportional-reasoning challenges (design, 2026-10-10)

Status: **Jeff's decisions are in (section 6) and the PILOT is built** — the shared Vine Ladder, Monkey Swap, Sunny Market, the jungle trophy stand + grid, the Achievements Wall board and Mills' welcome (section 8). The other eight challenges show as "coming soon" until Jeff has played the pilot. It builds on the Emerald Jungle land (see `claude/emerald-jungle-world.md`) and follows the Magma Multiples pattern (registry, stage frames, hosts, 15-round sets, local bests, trophy stand).

Jeff's brief: the next phase after additive (Snowball Sums) and multiplicative (Magma Multiples) thinking is **proportional reasoning**, built around the **double number line** in different contexts — unit conversions, speed/distance/time, rates, "if x of these is worth y of these" — where students work out values by scaling (double, halve, add, …). Students should interact with the proportional relationship, not just answer questions. It needs a trophy stand and a place on the Achievements Wall.

## 1. The big idea: one tool, ten places

Every challenge uses the **same** double number line, dressed for its context. Students meet it ten times — trading fruit, buying, racing, measuring, mixing, filling, mapping, graphing — and come to see that it is the same structure each time. That is the heart of proportional reasoning, and it is how a model grows from a "model of" one situation into a "model for" thinking (Gravemeijer's emergent modelling; Küchemann, Hodgen & Brown's ICCAMS work recommends using the DNL over time so it can emerge, not as a one-off procedure).

### The Vine Ladder (student name; teachers call it a double number line)
- Two vines strung side by side between posts, each labelled with ONE quantity and its unit (🍌 bananas / 🥭 mangoes; km / minutes; L / seconds).
- The two vines are **knotted together at a carved 0 post** — "tied at zero". This is the feature that makes it proportional, and it is shown, labelled and talked about (ICCAMS found worksheets that left the zeros off).
- A known pair is a **bamboo rung** across the two vines, with a number tag at each end.
- Mills' welcome tells students: "Explorers call this a **double number line**."

### Moves (unlocked gradually, card buttons + tap the 3D rungs, like Cart Tables' tablets)
| Move | What it does |
|---|---|
| Swap again / Repeat | Adds another copy of the starting rung (build-up: 3↔2, 6↔4, 9↔6…) |
| Double, Halve | New rung at 2× or ½ of the selected rung |
| × 10, ÷ 10 | Big jumps |
| Split into n | Chops 0→rung into n equal pieces on BOTH vines (the partitioning move; gives the unit or a friendly piece) |
| × n | Any whole-number stretch of a rung |
| Join, Gap | Add two rungs / subtract one from another (6↔4 + 9↔6 = 15↔10) |

The scene places each new rung and fills in the "driving" quantity; **the student types the partner number** (the Cart Tables rule: the scene never does the maths for them).

### What wrong looks like (the consequence is the feedback)
- **Crooked rung:** a wrong partner number is drawn where it really sits on the bottom vine, so the rung leans. Shake + try again, no dead end.
- **Untied zero:** if a student adds the same amount to both (3↔2 → 6↔5), the bottom vine has to slide to make that rung straight — and the zeros come apart. "Now 0 mangoes sits under 1 banana. Free fruit? Not a fair swap." This makes the additive error visible and explains WHY it's wrong (the detection is like Grow the Dragon's additive check).
- **Off the end of the vine:** the vines grow/rescale as rungs pass the end.
- Then each context adds its own world consequence (the race runs, the barrel overflows, the treasure is or isn't there).

### Predict first (where the DNL beats the ratio table)
Several challenges ask the student to **drop a marker where they think the answer lands** before calculating (between which rungs? nearer which?). The number line shows size and order, so estimating is natural; the prediction is then tested by the world consequence. Estimation points reward a close drop.

### The coupled bead (covariation)
A glowing bead can slide along the ladder showing both values at once — as one quantity grows, the other grows with it. Used openly early on (Waterfall Barrels), hidden later so students must reason.

## 2. Design principles (from the research and from Jeff's feedback on the other worlds)
1. **Along first, then across.** ICCAMS found students much better at "along the line" (scalar) reasoning than "between the lines" (the multiplier/rate): 91% vs 51% on the same task. So challenges 1–3 use along-the-line moves; challenge 4 makes the across multiplier its whole focus; later challenges use both and ask which is easier.
2. **Each line is one measure, clearly labelled, zeros shown.** (ICCAMS: mixed-measure lines confuse students.)
3. **Contexts across the curriculum.** Australian research (Hilton, Hilton, Dole & Goos) frames proportional reasoning as numeracy across subjects: money, science measurement, PE pace and heart rate, food tech recipes, geography maps. The ten contexts are chosen to cover that range.
4. **Know when NOT to scale.** Once students learn proportional methods they over-use them (De Bock, Van Dooren, Janssens & Verschaffel's "illusion of linearity"). One whole challenge (the Temple of Truth) is about telling proportional from non-proportional situations. **No trick rounds anywhere else** (Jeff, 2026-10-10) — not in the other sets and not in the capstone.
5. **The additive trap is named and seen** (Hart's CSMS study; Karplus et al.): the untied zero, the wrong potion colour.
6. **Platform conventions:** the scene owns the maths, the bottom-docked card owns the input; two-step intros + one worked example; no multiple choice where a manipulative works; no route previews that give the answer away; wrong = shake + try again; gold needs the smart way; 15 rounds (5 stages × 3), 25 pts a round, 375 max; gold 100% / silver 75% / bronze 50%; bests saved locally (`mma-jungle-<key>-best`).
7. **Builds on Magma, doesn't repeat it.** Cart Tables already does a ratio table with ×2 ÷2 ×10 ÷10 + −, and Grow the Dragon does scale factors and the add-the-same trap. The jungle adds what those don't: position and size on a line, zero alignment, continuous quantities (halves, decimals, money, time), two different units, rates, the link to graphs, and deciding when proportion applies.
8. **Metric only** (Jeff, 2026-10-10) — no imperial units anywhere.

### 1 · Monkey Swap — Palm Grove (Mr. Morgan) — BUILT
**Idea.** A ratio is a composed unit you can iterate (Lamon's unitising; Lobato & Ellis). Every fair swap sits on the same ladder. The DNL is "born" here.
**Scene.** A thatched fruit stall in the grove, monkeys on and beside the counter. The Vine Ladder is strung across the clearing: 🍌 on top, 🥭 below, tied at the 0 post. The deal sign reads "3 🍌 → 2 🥭". In the early stages, real fruit hangs along both vines as rungs are hung, so the equal spacing is visible; later only numbers remain (model of → model for).
**The student.** Builds rungs with Swap again / Double / × 10 / Join, typing the mangoes for each. When the target rung is reached, the deal lands and the monkeys cheer. A wrong rung goes crooked.
**Stages.** See section 8 for the pilot's five stages.
**Trap.** The "Add 3 to both" mistake is detected and shown as the untied zero.

### 2 · Sunny Market — Sunny Knoll (Mr. Pearce) — BUILT
**Idea.** The unitary method (NSW: "apply the unitary method"): split down to one — or to a friendlier piece — then build back up. Unit price is a rate. Best buys compare two rates.
**Scene.** Market stalls with striped awnings on the sunny hilltop with price boards. Ladder: fruit on top, dollars below.
**The student.** Split into n is the new move: it chops the segment from 0 to a rung into n equal parts on both vines; the student types one piece.
**Stages.** See section 8 for the pilot's five stages.
**Trap.** Choosing the bigger pack because it "looks cheaper", or comparing totals without a common quantity.

### 3 · Lagoon Race — Lily Lagoon (Mr. Heywood)
**Idea.** Speed is a rate (distance per time). To compare rates, line them up at a common time or a common distance — or find each per-second rate.
**Scene.** Racing lanes across the lagoon marked by lily pads every metre, three racers (frog, turtle, duck). Each racer's form card: "🐸 6 m every 4 s".
**The student.** Builds each racer's ladder (m ↔ s), drops a flag where they predict the racer will be, then the race runs and the racer stops at — or past — the flag.
**Stages.**
- S1 How far? — "The frog swims 6 m every 4 s. Where is it after 20 s?" (30 m). Drop the flag, build, race.
- S2 How long? — "How long to cross the 30 m lagoon?"
- S3 Who wins? — "Turtle 9 m in 6 s vs Duck 12 m in 10 s." Build both to a common time (30 s: 45 m vs 36 m) or a common distance (36 m: 24 s vs 30 s), call the winner, watch the race.
- S4 Metres each second — find each racer's per-second rung and compare (the across idea is previewed here).
- S5 Kayak tour — km and hours: "9 km in 1.5 h. How far in 4 h? What is its speed in km per hour?" (half hours on the time vine).
**Trap.** Comparing the given numbers directly ("12 is more than 9, so the duck wins").

### 4 · Tall Tree Tape — The Great Tree (Ms. Ewings)
**Idea.** When both lines measure the same thing in different units, every rung is a conversion, and there is ONE across multiplier that works at every rung (Vergnaud's function relation; the conversion factor). This is the hard direction (ICCAMS), so it gets a whole challenge.
**Scene.** A tape measure hangs from the treehouse deck (20 m up) down the trunk: one unit on each edge — a vertical double number line. **Metric only (Jeff, 2026-10-10)** — the awkward multipliers come from everyday metric measures instead of imperial units.
**The student.** Builds rungs along the tape, then is asked to find the arrow across ("What do you multiply by to go from metres to centimetres?"). The across arrow is tested on three rungs — it works every time — and then can be used as a move. Some rounds are quicker along, some across; the student chooses.
**Stages.**
- S1 Metric to metric — m ↔ cm, km ↔ m, L ↔ mL: the across arrow (× 100, × 1000) is easy to spot.
- S2 Kitchen measures — cups ↔ mL (1 cup = 250 mL), tablespoons ↔ mL (1 tbsp = 20 mL): find the across arrow × 250 / × 20 for a jungle recipe.
- S3 Paces ↔ metres — "8 paces ≈ 6 m" along the trail. Across is × 0.75 (awkward), so along moves win: the point is that BOTH ways work.
- S4 Up the tape — mixed metric units on one tape (m ↔ cm ↔ mm), and a monkey climbs the tape to the student's height — does it reach the deck?
- S5 Along or across? — mixed conversions (kg ↔ g, hours ↔ minutes, $ ↔ cents, mL ↔ L); the student picks the route.

### 5 · Mushroom Potions — Mushroom Glade (Ms. Brookes)
**Idea.** A ratio describes a mixture: the same ratio gives the same "taste" (here, colour), whatever the size of the batch. Adding the same amount to both parts changes the mixture (the additive trap again — Ms. Brookes' dragon callback). Part:part vs part:whole; dividing a quantity in a given ratio (NSW content).
**Scene.** A bubbling cauldron, jars of glowing blue moonberry and yellow sunfruit juice; the potion's green depends on the mix. A reference vial glows the target colour, and a "too blue / just right / too yellow" meter sits beside it (so colour is never the only cue).
**The student.** Scales the recipe on the ladder (scoops 🔵 ↔ scoops 🟡), then pours; the cauldron takes the colour of what was actually poured.
**Stages.**
- S1 Bigger batch — recipe 2 🔵 : 3 🟡, make it with 8 🔵.
- S2 Fern's potion — "Fern added 5 to each: 1 : 4 became 6 : 9. Same potion?" Predict, pour it (clearly too blue), then fix it on the ladder (6 : 24). (This is the challenge's own teaching about the additive mistake — every round is still a real proportion question.)
- S3 Which is bluer? — Noelting's orange-juice task: 3 🔵 : 5 🟡 vs 4 🔵 : 7 🟡. Build both to a common amount of blue (12: 20 vs 21 yellow), decide, pour both to check on the meter.
- S4 Fill the cauldron — a THIRD vine for the total (2 : 3 : 5): "The cauldron holds 30 scoops. How many of each?" (12 and 18).
- S5 Three ingredients — add a red ember-berry (2 : 3 : 1) or start from the total, a part, or the other part.
**Consequence.** A perfect potion lights up the glade's mushrooms (they glow and bounce higher for a while).

### 6 · Waterfall Barrels — Waterfall Pool (Trevor)
**Idea.** A rate is a ratio of two different measures ("per"; NSW: "explain the differences between ratios and rates", "convert between units for rates"). As time grows, the water grows with it (covariation).
**Scene.** A bamboo pipe channels the falls into wooden barrels with a litre gauge; a stopwatch. Ladder: seconds/minutes ↔ litres. The coupled time bead: drag it and the barrel's water level moves with it.
**The student.** Builds the ladder, sets the tap timer or the fill mark, then the tap runs (fast-forwarded): the barrel stops at the student's mark, falls short, or overflows with a splash.
**Stages.**
- S1 One minute — "4 L every 10 s. How much in a minute?" (24 L).
- S2 How long to fill? — "The barrel holds 50 L." Set the timer (125 s = 2 min 5 s). Too short → not full; too long → overflow.
- S3 Per minute, per hour — "3 L every 20 s. Litres per minute? Per hour?" (9 L/min, 540 L/h): regroup the time vine.
- S4 Two pipes — line both up at a common time and add (2 L per 5 s + 3 L per 10 s = 7 L per 10 s).
- S5 Rates everywhere — heartbeats ("18 beats in 15 s — beats per minute?"), drumbeats, ants carrying leaves per minute, rainfall in mm per hour.

### 7 · Treasure Map — Canopy Lookout (Ms. Mahoney)
**Idea.** Scale is a ratio; a scale bar is a ladder from map distance to real distance, and the across multiplier is the scale factor.
**Scene.** On the 26 m Canopy Lookout deck, a map table with an old explorer's map of the valley — and the real valley spread out below.
**The student.** Lays a string along the dotted route on the map (drag) to get the map distance, converts on the ladder (map cm ↔ real m), then a flare rises from the valley at the student's distance along the route. Is it on the glinting treasure?
**Stages.**
- S1 Simple scale — 1 cm = 10 m; routes of 3–9 cm.
- S2 Scale bars — "2 cm = 50 m" (7 cm → 175 m).
- S3 Backwards — "The trail is 175 m. How long is it on the map?"
- S4 Ratio scales — 1 : 2500 means 1 cm on the map is 2500 cm = 25 m (a unit change on one vine).
- S5 Long routes — 1 : 25 000 (4 cm = 1 km), two-leg routes.
**Reward idea.** The last round's X marks a real chest location in the valley that the student can then walk to and open.

### 8 · Firefly Graphs — Redwood Hollow (Mr. Dawson)
**Idea.** Stand the ladder up and it becomes a graph: every rung becomes a point, and a proportional ladder's points sit on a straight line through zero. Steeper = faster. Distance–time graphs (NSW: interpret, calculate speeds for segments, create).
**Scene.** The ladder lies along the ground; on "Stand it up" the distance vine swings up a giant redwood trunk, each rung becomes a firefly at (time, distance), and the fireflies line up along a vine that grows from the roots (the origin).
**The student.** Builds a ladder, stands it up, reads values off the line with a sliding firefly; later drags points to build graphs from stories while a walker acts the journey out on the trail.
**Stages.**
- S1 Stand it up — "2 km every 30 min"; stand it, then "How far after 75 min?" by sliding along the line.
- S2 Steeper is faster — two walkers' lines on one graph; match each to its walker (3 km in 45 min vs 4 km in 40 min), then they walk and the graph draws live.
- S3 Stops and turns — a rest is a flat segment, coming back slopes down. Match a graph to a story.
- S4 Speed from a segment — km per hour for each straight piece.
- S5 Draw the trip — "walked 3 km in 1 h, rested 30 min, walked 2 km in 30 min": drag the corners; the walker acts out the student's graph, mismatches are visible.

### 9 · Temple of Truth — Temple Ruins (Robot)
**Idea.** Discernment: not every "if x then y" situation is proportional (Lamon; Van Dooren et al.; Cramer, Post & Currier). Using the ladder where it doesn't belong gives wrong answers. This is the ONE place non-proportional situations appear (Jeff's "no trick rounds" decision) — and here they are the explicit, announced topic, not a trick.
**Scene.** The temple door is carved with tablets; each shows a jungle situation with two or three pairs of values. "Hang it on the vines": proportional data hangs straight and ties at zero; the rest won't.
**The student.** Hangs each tablet's pairs on the ladder, calls "Ladder works" or "Ladder breaks", and for a break picks the reason (tiles, not text): **starts with a fee** (canoe hire $10 + $5 an hour — doesn't tie at zero), **same difference** (Sam is 3 years older than his sister: 12 and 9 now, 21 and 18 later, not 24), **stays the same** (one bird sings its song in 3 minutes; five birds singing together still take 3), **grows faster and faster** (a square's side and its area), **°C and °F** (0 °C is 32 °F). For proportional tablets they then finish the missing value.
**Stages.** S1 one obvious each way · S2 starts with a fee (and still answer it: the ladder works on the part after the fee) · S3 same difference · S4 stays the same / grows faster · S5 mixed, five calls opens the door.
**Consequence.** Each true call lights a glyph; five lights swing the door open.

### 10 · Pathfinder — Sky Shrine (Ms. Bacon)
**Idea.** The capstone, mirroring Aurora Lookout and Strategy Summit: the same problem, three correct routes on the ladder — which is easiest? Then do it. Flexible, efficient proportional reasoning.
**Scene.** Three vine bridges lead across a gap to the shrine on the plateau; each carries one route.
**Example.** "6 km takes 90 min. How long for 14 km?" → (a) split to 1 km (15 min), × 14; (b) split to 2 km (30 min), × 7; (c) double to 12 km (180 min), join 2 km (30 min). All correct; the student chooses the easiest (+10, others +5), then does it (+15).
**Stages.** Contexts from all nine challenges in turn (swaps, prices, speeds, conversions, potions, flow, maps, graph readings). Every round is genuinely proportional — no "no ladder needed" trick rounds (Jeff's decision). S5 chains two ladders (map → metres → minutes at walking pace) — a three-line expedition plan.
**Bridge to Magma.** One route can be shown as Steve's cart table, so students see the ratio table and the double number line are the same idea.

## 7. Research spine
- NSW Mathematics K–10 (2022) Stage 4 **Ratios and rates, MA4-RAT-C-01** — "solves problems involving ratios and rates, and analyses distance–time graphs": unitary method, divide in a ratio, ratios vs rates, convert units for rates, best buys, distance–time graphs with positive, zero and negative slopes.
- Küchemann, Hodgen & Brown (2014), *The use of alternative double number lines as models of ratio tasks and as models for ratio relations and scaling* (ICCAMS, BCME8): along vs between the lines (91% vs 51%), label zeros, one measure per line, let the model emerge.
- Lamon (2012), *Teaching Fractions and Ratios for Understanding*: unitising and composed units, partitioning, relative thinking, discerning proportional from non-proportional situations.
- Lobato & Ellis (2010), NCTM *Essential Understanding of Ratios, Proportions and Proportional Reasoning, Grades 6–8*: iterating and partitioning composed units; covariation and invariance; unit rate.
- Vergnaud (1983, 1988): scalar (within-measure) and function (between-measure) relationships.
- Noelting (1980): the orange-juice mixture comparison tasks.
- Hart (1981), CSMS *Children's Understanding of Mathematics: 11–16*; Karplus, Pulos & Stage (1983): the additive strategy in proportion problems.
- De Bock, Van Dooren, Janssens & Verschaffel (2007), *The Illusion of Linearity*; Van Dooren et al. (2009), JRME 40(2): over-use of proportional methods.
- Hilton, Hilton, Dole & Goos (2013), MERJ — a two-tier diagnostic of middle-years proportional reasoning; (2016) ESM — proportional reasoning across the curriculum.
- Gravemeijer (1999) — emergent models ("model of" → "model for"); Fosnot & Dolk — the double number line as a tool for rates and percents.
- Siemon, Bleckly & Neal (2012) — proportional reasoning as the big idea that follows multiplicative thinking (Magma's spine).

## 8. The pilot as built (2026-10-10)

**Where to find it.** Monkey Swap is in the Palm Grove (Mr. Morgan beside the ladder); Sunny Market is on top of Sunny Knoll (Mr. Pearce). Mills meets arrivals on the glade and explains the vine ladder; the trophy stand is front-left of the arrival spot and opens the jungle trophy grid (two trophies live, eight "coming soon"). The Achievements Wall's Emerald Jungle board is now a trophy panel with the same eight "coming soon" slots.

**The Vine Ladder (shared by every challenge).** Two vines knotted at a 0 post; the starting deal is the first rung. Tap a rung (or two), pick a move — One more swap / Again, × 2, ÷ 2, × 10, ÷ 10, Split…, Times…, Join two, Gap — and the scene places the new rung and fills in one end; the student TYPES the other end. A wrong number hangs a crooked rung; adding the same to both unties the zero (the bottom vine slides, "0 ← slid!"). Moves that would need part of a fruit or part of a cent are refused with a nudge ("monkeys only swap WHOLE fruit", "try a friendlier piece!"). The ladder rescales as rungs pass its end; an off-scale target shows as a flag at the end post. Up to 8 new rungs a round.

**Scoring (25 a round, 375 a set; gold 100%, silver 75%, bronze 50%).** Reaching the target: 15 (−3 per wrong number, never below 6) + efficiency against the fewest possible moves: 10 / 6 / 3. Smallest-swap rounds: the same, −5 for each early "that's the smallest" claim. Best-buy rounds: 10 for building both ladders to a common amount (−2 per miss, min 4), +15 for picking the better stall.

**Monkey Swap (5 stages × 3).** S1 build it up (repeat only; real fruit hangs on the vines) · S2 jump ahead (× 2, × 10, join; targets ≤ 100) · S3 backwards (the target is on the mango vine) · S4 half a swap (even deals; the target needs ÷ 2) · S5 the smallest swap (a big offer like 12 🍌 → 8 🥭; split/halve down until it won't split into whole fruit, then claim it). Not built from the proposal: the "is 8 → 6 the same deal?" judgement round.

**Sunny Market (5 stages × 3).** S1 go through one (4 🍍 = $10 → 7 🍍) · S2 a friendlier piece (ONE doesn't come out in whole cents, so split into a bigger piece) · S3 how many can I buy? (the target is in dollars) · S4 best buy by count · S5 best buy by the kilo. Best buys use a THREE-vine ladder — Stall A's dollars above, the fruit in the middle, Stall B's dollars below — so a common amount of fruit lines up straight down both; the stall with more fruit always costs more in total (the "bigger looks cheaper" trap). Not built from the proposal: the shopping-monkey consequence (a verdict chip shows instead).

**Not in the pilot.** Predict-first markers, the coupled bead, Farsi/Arabic strings for the new text, and the other eight challenges.
