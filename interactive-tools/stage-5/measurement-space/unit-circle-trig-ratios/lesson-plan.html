Introducing the Unit Circle — ELPSA Lesson Plan

Students extend sine, cosine and tangent past 90° by watching a 1-unit stick turn on the number plane, noticing the patterns before any rule is named. A first lesson for Stage 5, built around the The Unit Circle and the Trigonometric Ratios tool, with notice-and-wonder moments and quick mini-whiteboard checks throughout.

Duration: 60 min
MA5-TRG-P-02 (Trigonometry D)
Mini-whiteboards · on-screen flip cards
E · Experience10 min

Exit ticket4 min

Learning intention LI

  • We are learning how sine, cosine and tangent work for angles bigger than 90°.
Assumed knowledge: SOH CAH TOA in right-angled triangles; plotting points on the number plane.

Success criteria SC

  • I can explain why, on a unit circle, sin θ is the y-coordinate and cos θ is the x-coordinate.
  • I can sketch the stick for any angle up to 360° and say which quadrant it is in.
  • I can decide whether sin, cos or tan of an angle is positive, negative or zero, and say why.
Tool set-up and shortcuts Before the lesson
Open The Unit Circle and the Trigonometric Ratios (Stage 5 › Measurement & Space). Leave every Settings layer off (they start off), side labels on Letters, graph panel open.
→ / ← next / back a step  •  ↑ ↓ turn the stick 1° (Shift: 15°)  •  L labels: letters / numbers / names  •  G graph panel  •  F full screen  •  Space start again  •  type any angle in the θ box
Materials: mini-whiteboards and markers, the on-screen flip cards (tool’s Tools menu › Flip cards, or /flip-cards/unit-circle-related-angles.html) — or the printable cards on the last page — and exit ticket slips.

Experience — a stick that turns E · 10 min

Start from what students know and something they can picture. Say very little; let them notice.
  1. Hook (2 min). “What things in your life turn round and round?” Collect a few: Ferris wheel, bike wheel, clock hands, the Earth. “Today we find out what trigonometry says about things that turn.”
  2. The stick (1 min). Step 1 of the tool: “Here is a stick. It is exactly 1 unit long. If one end stays still and the other turns, what shape does the free end trace?” (a circle)
  3. The first quadrant (2 min). Press Next: the stick lands on the plane and turns to 40°. “Where have you seen this triangle before?” (SOH CAH TOA). “The hypotenuse is 1. So what is sin 40° here?” Pairs work it out: opposite ÷ 1, the height of the point.
  4. Notice and wonder (5 min). Drag the stick slowly 0° → 90° and back while the three graphs draw and wind back. Whiteboards: one notice, one wonder each. Share four or five and write their words on the board exactly as said — they become the Language phase.
Likely noticings
  • sin gets bigger as cos gets smaller; at 45° they are equal
  • tan shoots up near 90°; the graphs are curves, not straight lines
Teacher move: don’t correct or name anything yet. If no one wonders about it, ask “The stick could keep turning. What would happen to sin after 90°?” Students commit a prediction on their whiteboard and keep it — it is answered in the Pictorial phase.

Language — from their words to ours L · 8 min

Turn the class’s noticings into shared vocabulary, linking each term to something a student said. Build a word wall as you go.
Their words (likely)The term we usePoint at on the tool
“the circle the stick draws”unit circle (radius 1, centre at the origin)the dashed circle
“how far the point is up”the point’s y-coordinate, which is sin θthe vertical side
“how far across it is”the point’s x-coordinate, which is cos θthe horizontal side
“the angle from the flat line”θ, measured anticlockwise from the positive x-axisthe arc at the origin
“the four corners of the graph”quadrants 1 to 4, numbered anticlockwisethe axes
Key sentence to say and repeat: “On the unit circle, cos is across and sin is up.”
  • Why it works (1 min). The hypotenuse is 1, so opposite ÷ hypotenuse is just the opposite side — the height of the point. Show it with side labels on Numbers, then back to Letters (L).
  • Quick check (2 min, whiteboards). Call out “the y-coordinate”, “cos”, “the vertical side”, “across”. Students write sin or cos. Re-teach anyone mixing up across and up, using the stick.

Pictorial — the whole circle P · 15 min

Students check their past-90° predictions against the picture, then learn to sketch the stick and read signs from it.
  1. The reveal (3 min). Press Next to zoom out to the whole unit circle; the graphs stretch to 360°. Drag to 120° and stop. “Look at your prediction. What happened to sin? To cos?” Whiteboards up. Then turn right round to 360° while the graphs draw.
  2. Second notice and wonder (4 min). One notice each. Look for: the side labels gain signs (−x left of the y-axis, −y below the x-axis); sin is positive above the x-axis and cos to the right of the y-axis; the graphs repeat their shapes, mirrored or flipped; tan goes off the graph at 90° and 270°.
    Key question: “The triangle’s sides are lengths. How can a length be negative?” Steer to: the sides aren’t negative — the coordinates are. The point at 150° is left of the origin, so its x-coordinate is negative.
  3. Quadrant check (2 min). Call 200°, 95°, 300°, 170°, 45°: students hold up 1–4 fingers. Repeat any that split the room, dragging the stick there.
  4. Sketch the stick (6 min). On whiteboards: a small circle with axes, then the stick and triangle for 150°, 240°, 330°, with + or − on the horizontal and vertical sides. Reveal each by dragging the stick there in Letters mode.
Look for: angles measured from the y-axis or turned clockwise; triangles drawn to the y-axis. Fix it now: the triangle always drops (or rises) to the x-axis.

Symbolic — writing it down S · 15 min

Name what the pictures showed, then practise it in symbols with the flip cards.
sin θ = y      cos θ = x      tan θ = y ⁄ x
  • Notes for books (4 min). The point on the unit circle at angle θ is (cos θ, sin θ) — turn on Settings › label the point to show it. The sign of each ratio comes from the signs of x and y in that quadrant. Have students build this table from their sketches, not copy it.
Quadrantx (cos)y (sin)y ÷ x (tan)
1 (0° to 90°)+++
2 (90° to 180°)−+−
3 (180° to 270°)−−+
4 (270° to 360°)+−−
If you use ASTC (All Stations To Central), introduce it here as a memory aid for a table they have just built — never as the starting point.
  • Flip cards (6 min). Open the on-screen flip cards (Tools › Flip cards) on set 1 · Positive or negative? with Settings › Related angles on 30°, 45°, 60° and Include 0°, 90°, 180°… on. Show a card, start the think-time ring (T), students sketch the stick first, then write +, − or 0 and hold up. Press Enter to flip: the back shows the sign, the quadrant and a small unit circle. Thumbs up / down / sideways is faster for the last few. (No projector? The printable cards are on the last page; answers in the table below.)
  • The equivalent statement (3 min, if they are flying). Press 2 for set Second quadrant: now the card says sin 150° and the answer is sin 30°, not just “+”. Two or three cards, then 5 All four quadrants (sin 210° = −sin 30°). This is next lesson’s idea, the related angle, met as a picture first.
  • Hinge question (3 min). Whiteboards, letter only: Which of these is negative?  A  sin 150°  ·  B  cos 300°  ·  C  tan 225°  ·  D  cos 120°
Reading the hinge answers
  • D is correct.
  • Many choose B: they have mixed up sin and cos → back to “cos is across”.
  • Many choose C: they missed that negative ÷ negative is positive → back to the quadrant table.
Tan at 90° (2 min)
  • Drag the stick to 90°: the tool says tan 90° is undefined.
  • “Why?” — x = 0, and you can’t divide by 0. Point to the dashed asymptote on the tan graph.

Application — where it turns up A · 8 min

Close with one real situation and a final notice and wonder, so the graph becomes a picture of something real.
  1. The Ferris wheel (4 min). More applications › The Ferris wheel: the circle becomes an 18 m wheel and a rider climbs in. Before it turns: “Sketch what you think their height from the centre will look like over time.” Whiteboards, then press Sweep and compare with the height graph.
  2. Final notice and wonder (4 min). “What do you notice about the rider’s height graph and the sin graph from earlier?” (same shape, 9 times taller). “What do you wonder?” Praise: “Does this work for other things that turn?” If time allows, flick through The tide, Sunrise over the year or The pendulum without explaining — a teaser for next lesson.

Exit ticket 4 min

  1. Sketch the stick at 240° and label the signs of x and y.
  2. Is cos 240° positive or negative? How do you know?
  3. Find another angle with the same sine as 240°.
Sort the slips into three piles: sign + reason · sign, no reason · sign wrong. Question 3 (300°) shows who is ready for related angles next lesson.

Misconceptions to listen for Watch

  • Treating the triangle’s side lengths as negative, rather than the coordinates.
  • Measuring the angle from the y-axis, or turning clockwise.
  • Swapping sin and cos (which is x, which is y).
  • Thinking tan 90° is 0, or “just very big”.

Differentiation Support & extend

Support
  • Give a printed unit circle with the quadrants numbered to sketch on.
  • Keep side labels on Names (Opposite / Adjacent) to bridge from SOH CAH TOA.
  • Do the flip cards for sin and cos only (flip cards Settings › Ratios); leave tan for later.
  • Keep Unit circle sketch on the back on, and ask them to copy the sketch before writing the sign.
Extend
  • Which two angles between 0° and 360° have sin θ = 0.5? How do you know from the circle?
  • Why does tan repeat every 180°, not every 360°?
  • Turn on angles in radians in the tool and redo three flip cards.
  • Flip cards set Solve: find θ (sin θ = −sin 40° → 220°, 320°) or Beyond 360° and negative angles.
