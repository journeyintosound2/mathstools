Division with a Ratio Table

MA3-MR-01

Every question starts with one row: 1 group, and what one group is worth. A new row is entered the way a coordinate is — type the groups, press Enter, then type the total for those groups. Get the pair right and it locks in; get it wrong and the whole pair shakes and goes, because half a row is not a row. Rows can also be built from rows already there: ×10, halve it, add two together, take one off another — and the move is flashed on both columns, which is the whole claim a ratio table makes. When a row lands exactly on the number being divided, the left column is the answer: 270 ÷ 15 = 18. Optional Cartesian plane plots every row as a point, so a Stage 3 table and a Stage 4 straight line through the origin are the same picture.

Division by Grouping

MA3-MR-01

Type how many go in each group and the number flies to the first bubble, then copies itself into every other one while the tally counts up — skip counting you can see. Overshoot the target and it flies straight back out. Because the groups are filled a chunk at a time, the chunks add to the answer: 10 and then 4 is 84 ÷ 6 = 14. Two- and three-digit numbers, optional remainders and base-ten counters.

Addition by Compensation

MA3-AR-01

Each number is a pile of tiles standing in columns of ten, with the unfinished tens facing each other and their empty spaces dashed in. Drag a tile (or a selected group) from one pile to the other and the statement above changes: 36 + 49 becomes 35 + 50, with matching −1 and +1 chips. Moved tiles keep their colour, so a class can see where every tile went. The original question stays underneath, and the total stays hidden until you show it.

Halve and Halve Again

MA3-MR-01

A bar holding the whole number is cut in half, and in half again. The amount inside swells, pops, and two equal halves fall into place — so a class watches one amount become two, not two new numbers appear. Halving twice gives four equal parts, three times gives eight, and the last press lifts one part out and lays it opposite the equals sign, because the answer is what one part is worth. A halving ladder writes each step down beside it, and the check doubles back up the same ladder. ÷ 2, 4 or 8, two- or three-digit numbers, and an optional wall that keeps every stage on screen at once.
